Foundations of Artificial Intelligence

A one-semester introduction to artificial intelligence, from classical search and logic to the neural networks and large language models you run on your own hardware.

This course follows the shape of a typical first AI module at a European university: a weekly lecture, a weekly exercise session with problem sheets, a set of programming labs, and a written exam at the end. Every lecture comes as a self-contained script with learning objectives, numbered definitions and examples, a summary and exercises with model solutions. After completing the module, students are able to:

Lecture 1 · Week 1 · Part I Classical AI

What is AI? Agents and environments

Four definitions of AI, a short history in three waves, and the one framework this whole course uses: the rational agent.

Learning objectives

  • Distinguish the four classic views of AI (thinking/acting × humanly/rationally).
  • Place symbolic AI, machine learning and deep learning on a timeline and explain why each wave rose and stalled.
  • Describe a task as an agent with a PEAS specification and classify its environment.
  • Name the main agent architectures, from simple reflex to learning agents.

1.1 Four ways to define AI

There is no single accepted definition of artificial intelligence. Russell and Norvig sort the historical definitions along two axes: whether they concern thinking or behaviour, and whether they measure success against human performance or against an ideal of rationality.

Human-likeRational
ThinkingCognitive modelling: build programs whose reasoning resembles human reasoning (cognitive science).Laws of thought: derive correct conclusions with formal logic.
ActingThe Turing test: behave so that a judge cannot tell machine from human.Rational agents: act to achieve the best expected outcome.

This course takes the bottom-right view. A rational agent is easier to define precisely than "human-like thinking", it covers logic as one tool among several, and it is how practitioners actually build systems: you state what counts as success and design the system to maximise it.

Turing 1950. Turing proposed his "imitation game" to replace the question "Can machines think?", which he considered too vague to be meaningful. Modern LLMs pass casual versions of it, which says more about the test than about thinking.

1.2 A short history in three waves

Symbolic AI (1956 – 1990s)

The 1956 Dartmouth workshop named the field. Early successes in theorem proving, game playing and toy-world planning led to great optimism. Knowledge was written by hand as symbols and rules; expert systems of the 1980s encoded thousands of if-then rules from human experts. They were brittle: every situation the rule-writers had not foreseen broke them, and maintaining large rule bases proved too expensive. Funding collapsed twice, in the so-called AI winters of the mid-1970s and late 1980s.

Statistical machine learning (1990s – 2012)

Instead of writing rules, systems learned them from data: spam filters, speech recognisers, recommender systems. Probabilistic methods (Bayesian networks, hidden Markov models) and kernel methods (support vector machines) dominated. Features still had to be designed by hand.

Deep learning (2012 – today)

In 2012 a convolutional network trained on GPUs (AlexNet) won the ImageNet competition by a wide margin. Neural networks learn their own features from raw data, and they keep improving with more data and compute. The Transformer (2017) made it possible to train very large language models, and since about 2022 these models are good enough to be general-purpose assistants and agents.

Example 1.1 (Three waves on one task)

Recognising handwritten digits. Symbolic: rules such as "a 1 is a vertical stroke". Statistical ML: hand-designed features (stroke counts, pixel histograms) fed to an SVM. Deep learning: raw pixels fed to a CNN that learns the features itself, with error rates below 0.3% on MNIST.

1.3 Agents

Definition 1.1 (Agent)

An agent is anything that perceives its environment through sensors and acts upon it through actuators. The sequence of everything it has perceived so far is its percept sequence. Its behaviour is described by the agent function, a mapping from percept sequences to actions, and implemented by an agent program.

Definition 1.2 (Rational agent)

For each possible percept sequence, a rational agent selects an action that is expected to maximise its performance measure, given the evidence of the percept sequence and whatever built-in knowledge it has.

Rational is not the same as omniscient or perfect. A rational agent can make a decision that turns out badly, as long as it was the best decision given what it knew. Rationality also includes information gathering: looking before crossing the road is the rational action.

PEAS: specifying a task

Before designing an agent, write down its task environment as PEAS: Performance measure, Environment, Actuators, Sensors.

Example 1.2 (PEAS for a coding agent)

A coding agent such as Claude Code running on a local LLM:

  • P: tests pass, the user's request is met, few wasted steps, no destructive actions.
  • E: a source repository, a shell, the user, the model server.
  • A: edit files, run commands, write messages to the user.
  • S: file contents, command output, user messages.

Environment types

Environments differ along several dimensions, and each one affects which methods work:

  • Fully vs. partially observable: can the sensors see the complete relevant state? Chess: fully. Poker, driving: partially.
  • Deterministic vs. stochastic: is the next state fully determined by the current state and action?
  • Episodic vs. sequential: do current decisions affect future ones? Classifying one image: episodic. Chess: sequential.
  • Static vs. dynamic: does the world change while the agent deliberates?
  • Discrete vs. continuous: states, time, percepts and actions.
  • Single vs. multi-agent: are there other agents, cooperative or competitive?
  • Known vs. unknown: does the agent know the rules (the transition model) of the environment?

The hardest case is partially observable, stochastic, sequential, dynamic, continuous, multi-agent and unknown. Driving a taxi is the textbook example.

1.4 Agent architectures

  1. Simple reflex agent: acts on the current percept only, with condition–action rules. Works only in fully observable environments.
  2. Model-based reflex agent: keeps an internal state that tracks the parts of the world it cannot currently see.
  3. Goal-based agent: considers the future: "what will happen if I do this, and does it get me to my goal?" This needs search and planning (lectures 2–3).
  4. Utility-based agent: replaces a yes/no goal with a utility function that rates how desirable a state is, so it can trade off conflicting goals and uncertainty.
  5. Learning agent: any of the above, plus a learning element that improves the agent from feedback. A critic judges performance, a problem generator suggests exploratory actions.

Today's LLM agents fit this scheme: the LLM is the agent program, the context window is the internal state, and tool calls are the actuators. Lecture 13 returns to this.

Summary

  • We define AI as the study of rational agents: systems that act to maximise expected performance.
  • AI has gone through symbolic, statistical and deep-learning waves; each solved its predecessor's main bottleneck.
  • PEAS and the environment dimensions tell you which methods a task needs.

  1. Write a PEAS description for (a) a robot vacuum cleaner, (b) a spam filter, (c) a chess engine. [6 pts]
    Solution

    (a) P: floor area cleaned, energy used, collisions, time. E: rooms, furniture, dirt, people, pets. A: wheels, brushes, suction. S: bump sensors, cliff sensors, lidar or camera, dirt sensor, battery level.

    (b) P: accuracy, with false positives (good mail marked spam) weighted much higher than false negatives. E: incoming mail stream, senders, users. A: label as spam / not spam, move to folder. S: message headers, body text, attachments, user feedback.

    (c) P: games won, drawn, lost; Elo rating; time used. E: board, opponent, clock. A: moves. S: board state, opponent's moves, clock.

  2. Classify each environment from 1.1 along the seven dimensions of section 1.3. [6 pts]
    Solution

    Vacuum: partially observable, stochastic, sequential, dynamic, continuous, single-agent (people are part of the environment), partly known. Spam filter: partially observable (sender intent hidden), stochastic, episodic per message (though spammers adapt over time), static per decision, discrete, multi-agent in the adversarial sense (spammers), known. Chess: fully observable, deterministic, sequential, semi-dynamic (the clock runs), discrete, multi-agent competitive, known.

  3. "A rational agent never makes mistakes." True or false? Justify with an example. [3 pts]
    Solution

    False. Rationality maximises expected performance given the available evidence. An agent that crosses an empty road after looking both ways acts rationally even if a meteorite then hits it; the outcome was not foreseeable from its percepts.

  4. Why did the expert systems of the 1980s fail to scale? Name two reasons and say how machine learning addresses each. [4 pts]
    Solution

    (1) The knowledge-acquisition bottleneck: rules had to be elicited from experts by hand, which was slow and expensive. ML learns the mapping from examples instead. (2) Brittleness: rules failed silently outside the situations their authors foresaw, and rule interactions became unmanageable. Learned models generalise statistically and degrade more gracefully, and they can be retrained when the data changes.

Lecture 2 · Week 2 · Part I Classical AI

Problem solving by search

When an agent knows how the world works, it can plan by searching through possible action sequences. This lecture covers the formal setup and the core algorithms, from breadth-first search to A*.

Learning objectives

  • Formulate a problem as a search problem: states, actions, transition model, goal test, path cost.
  • Run BFS, DFS, uniform-cost search and A* by hand and state their properties.
  • Design admissible and consistent heuristics and prove that A* is then optimal.

2.1 Search problems

Definition 2.1 (Search problem)

A search problem consists of: a set of states \(S\); an initial state \(s_0\); for each state, a set of applicable actions \(A(s)\); a transition model \(\mathrm{Result}(s,a)\); a goal test; and an action cost \(c(s,a,s') \ge 0\). A solution is a sequence of actions from \(s_0\) to a goal state; an optimal solution has the lowest total path cost.

The states and actions implicitly define a graph, the state space. It is usually far too large to write down: the 15-puzzle has about \(10^{13}\) states, chess about \(10^{44}\). Search algorithms therefore build a search tree on the fly, expanding one node at a time. The set of generated but not yet expanded nodes is the frontier. Algorithms differ only in which frontier node they expand next.

Example 2.1 (Route finding)

States: cities. Actions: drive along a road to a neighbouring city. Cost: kilometres. Goal: arrive in Munich. The state space is the road map itself.

Measuring algorithms

We judge search algorithms by four criteria: completeness (does it always find a solution if one exists?), optimality (does it find a cheapest one?), and time and space complexity, expressed with the branching factor \(b\) (maximum number of successors), the depth \(d\) of the shallowest solution and the maximum depth \(m\).

2.2 Uninformed search

Uninformed (blind) strategies use only the problem definition.

  • Breadth-first search (BFS) expands the shallowest node first, using a FIFO queue. It is complete, and optimal when all action costs are equal. Time and space are both \(O(b^d)\); in practice memory runs out first.
  • Uniform-cost search (UCS, Dijkstra's algorithm) expands the node with the lowest path cost \(g(n)\), using a priority queue. It is complete and optimal for positive costs.
  • Depth-first search (DFS) expands the deepest node first, using a stack. It needs only \(O(bm)\) memory but is neither complete (infinite paths) nor optimal.
  • Iterative deepening runs depth-limited DFS with limits 0, 1, 2, … It combines DFS's small memory with BFS's completeness and optimality for unit costs. Re-expanding the shallow levels costs surprisingly little, because most nodes are in the deepest level.

Graph vs. tree search. Keeping a set of already reached states avoids exploring the same state twice through different paths. Without it, even a small grid produces an exponential tree.

2.3 Informed search and A*

Definition 2.2 (Heuristic)

A heuristic \(h(n)\) estimates the cost of the cheapest path from node \(n\) to a goal. It is admissible if it never overestimates: \(h(n) \le h^*(n)\) for the true cost \(h^*\). It is consistent if \(h(n) \le c(n,a,n') + h(n')\) for every successor \(n'\) (a triangle inequality).

Greedy best-first search expands the node with the smallest \(h(n)\). It is fast but not optimal: it chases whatever looks closest. A* search expands the node with the smallest

\[ f(n) = g(n) + h(n), \]

the estimated cost of the cheapest solution through \(n\).

Theorem 2.1 (Optimality of A*)

With an admissible heuristic, A* tree search is optimal. With a consistent heuristic, A* graph search is optimal, and it expands every node at most once.

Proof sketch. Suppose a suboptimal goal \(G_2\) is on the frontier, and let \(n\) be a frontier node on an optimal path to the optimal goal \(G\) with cost \(C^*\). Then \(f(n) = g(n) + h(n) \le C^*\) by admissibility, while \(f(G_2) = g(G_2) > C^*\) since \(h(G_2) = 0\). So \(n\) is expanded before \(G_2\), and by induction the whole optimal path is expanded first. \(\square\)

Example 2.2 (Heuristics for the 8-puzzle)

\(h_1\): number of misplaced tiles. \(h_2\): sum of the Manhattan distances of the tiles from their goal squares. Both are admissible, because every move fixes at most one tile by at most one square. Since \(h_2(n) \ge h_1(n)\) for all \(n\), \(h_2\) dominates \(h_1\) and A* with \(h_2\) never expands more nodes. At solution depth 24, A* with \(h_1\) expands about 39,000 nodes, with \(h_2\) about 1,600.

Good heuristics often come from relaxed problems: remove a constraint and solve the easier problem exactly. If tiles could move to any adjacent square even when occupied, the exact solution cost would be \(h_2\).

Summary

  • Search algorithms differ only in the order they expand frontier nodes.
  • BFS and UCS are complete and optimal but memory-hungry; DFS is frugal but neither; iterative deepening gets the best of both for unit costs.
  • A* with \(f = g + h\) is optimal for admissible (tree) or consistent (graph) heuristics. Better heuristics mean far fewer expansions.

Exercises (Sheet 2)

  1. A graph has nodes S, A, B, C, G with edges S–A (1), S–B (4), A–B (2), A–C (5), B–C (1), C–G (3). Heuristic: h(S)=5, h(A)=4, h(B)=3, h(C)=2, h(G)=0. Run UCS and A* from S to G. Give the expansion order and the path found. [6 pts]
    Solution

    UCS: expand S (0); frontier A(1), B(4). Expand A (1); B improves to 3 via A, C(6). Expand B (3); C improves to 4. Expand C (4); G(7). Expand G (7). Order S, A, B, C, G. Path S–A–B–C–G, cost 7.

    A*: f(S)=5. Expand S; A: f=1+4=5, B: f=4+3=7. Expand A; B via A: f=3+3=6, C: f=6+2=8. Expand B (6); C via B: f=4+2=6. Expand C (6); G: f=7+0=7. Expand G. Same order and path, cost 7. Check: h is admissible (true costs to G: S 7, A 6, B 4, C 3).

  2. Show that the straight-line distance is an admissible heuristic for route finding on a road map. Is it consistent? [3 pts]
    Solution

    No road between two points is shorter than the straight line, so it never overestimates. It is also consistent: by the triangle inequality, the straight-line distance from n to the goal is at most the road length from n to n′ plus the straight-line distance from n′ to the goal.

  3. Why is iterative deepening's overhead small? Compute the number of generated nodes for b = 10, d = 5 and compare with BFS. [4 pts]
    Solution

    Level i is generated (d − i + 1) times: N(IDS) = 5·10 + 4·100 + 3·1,000 + 2·10,000 + 1·100,000 = 123,450. BFS generates 10 + 100 + 1,000 + 10,000 + 100,000 = 111,110. The overhead is about 11%, because the last level dominates.

  4. Is h(n) = max(h₁(n), h₂(n)) admissible if h₁ and h₂ are? Is it better than each? [2 pts]
    Solution

    Yes: both are ≤ h*, so their maximum is too. It dominates both, so A* expands no more nodes than with either one, at the price of computing both.

Lecture 3 · Week 3 · Part I Classical AI

Adversarial search and constraint satisfaction

Two extensions of search: playing against an opponent who wants you to lose, and problems where the goal is a set of constraints rather than a path.

Learning objectives

  • Compute minimax values and apply alpha-beta pruning by hand.
  • Explain evaluation functions and Monte Carlo tree search.
  • Formulate a CSP and solve it with backtracking, variable ordering and arc consistency.

3.1 Games as search

In a two-player, zero-sum game with perfect information (chess, Go, tic-tac-toe), one player, MAX, tries to maximise the final score and the other, MIN, tries to minimise it. A game is defined by an initial state, the player to move, the legal actions, a transition model, a terminal test and a utility for terminal states.

Definition 3.1 (Minimax value)

The minimax value of a state is its utility if it is terminal; otherwise the maximum of its successors' values if MAX is to move, and the minimum if MIN is to move:

\[ \mathrm{MM}(s) = \begin{cases} \mathrm{Utility}(s) & \text{terminal} \\ \max_a \mathrm{MM}(\mathrm{Result}(s,a)) & \text{MAX to move} \\ \min_a \mathrm{MM}(\mathrm{Result}(s,a)) & \text{MIN to move} \end{cases} \]

Minimax is optimal against an optimal opponent, but it visits the whole tree: \(O(b^m)\) time. Chess has \(b \approx 35\) and games last about 80 plies, so \(35^{80}\) is out of the question.

Alpha-beta pruning

Alpha-beta pruning computes the same value while skipping branches that cannot affect the decision. It tracks \(\alpha\), the best value MAX can already guarantee on the current path, and \(\beta\), the best value MIN can guarantee. When a node's value is known to be worse than an alternative already available to the player above it (\(\alpha \ge \beta\)), the rest of its children are pruned.

With perfect move ordering, alpha-beta examines \(O(b^{m/2})\) nodes, which doubles the searchable depth in the same time. Good move ordering (captures first, the best move from the previous iteration first) gets close to that.

Example 3.1 (Pruning)

MAX at the root has two MIN children. The first MIN node's leaves are 3, 12, 8, so its value is 3 and \(\alpha = 3\). The second MIN node's first leaf is 2. Since MIN will choose at most 2 there, and MAX already has 3, the remaining leaves of the second node are never examined.

Cutting off search

Real programs stop at a depth limit and apply an evaluation function that estimates the utility of a non-terminal position: in chess classically a weighted sum of material, mobility and king safety, today often a neural network. Monte Carlo tree search (MCTS) takes another approach: it estimates a position's value from the results of many simulated games (playouts) and focuses its tree on promising moves, balancing exploration and exploitation with the UCB1 formula. AlphaGo (2016) and AlphaZero combined MCTS with neural networks that predict values and good moves.

3.2 Constraint satisfaction problems

Definition 3.2 (CSP)

A constraint satisfaction problem consists of variables \(X_1,\dots,X_n\), a domain \(D_i\) of possible values for each, and a set of constraints, each restricting the allowed combinations of values for a subset of variables. A solution is a complete assignment that satisfies all constraints.

Example 3.2 (Map colouring)

Colour the German states with red, green and blue so that neighbouring states differ. Variables: the 16 states. Domains: {R, G, B}. Constraints: \(X_i \ne X_j\) for every pair of neighbours. Sudoku, timetabling and register allocation are CSPs too.

CSPs have structure that general search ignores: the order of assignments does not matter, and a violated constraint can be detected as soon as its variables are assigned. Backtracking search assigns one variable at a time and backs up as soon as a constraint is violated. Three heuristics make it much faster:

  • Minimum remaining values (MRV): choose the variable with the fewest legal values left ("fail first").
  • Degree heuristic: break ties by choosing the variable involved in the most constraints with unassigned variables.
  • Least constraining value: try first the value that rules out the fewest values for neighbours.

Constraint propagation

Forward checking removes, after each assignment, inconsistent values from the neighbours' domains. Arc consistency goes further: an arc \(X \to Y\) is consistent if for every value of \(X\) there is some allowed value of \(Y\). The AC-3 algorithm repeatedly removes values that violate this until nothing changes, often detecting failure long before search would. Sudoku puzzles for newspapers can usually be solved by propagation alone.

Summary

  • Minimax gives optimal play in zero-sum games; alpha-beta finds the same answer with far fewer nodes.
  • Real game programs cut off search with evaluation functions or use MCTS, today guided by neural networks.
  • CSPs are solved by backtracking plus smart ordering and constraint propagation.

Exercises (Sheet 3)

  1. A MAX root has three MIN children with leaves (3, 12, 8), (2, 4, 6), (14, 5, 2), examined left to right. Compute the minimax value and list the leaves alpha-beta prunes. [5 pts]
    Solution

    MIN values: 3, 2, 2. Root value: 3. Pruned: 4 and 6 (after seeing 2 in the second node, which is below α = 3). In the third node, 14 and 5 must be examined (both above 3); only after 2 is the node known to be ≤ 2. Nothing is pruned there since 2 is the last leaf.

  2. Reorder the children in 3.1 so that alpha-beta prunes as much as possible. How many leaves are examined? [3 pts]
    Solution

    Put the best MIN node first and, inside each other node, its smallest leaf first: (3, 12, 8), (2, 4, 6), (2, 14, 5). The first node needs all 3 leaves; the others are cut after their first leaf: 3 + 1 + 1 = 5 leaves.

  3. Formulate 4-queens as a CSP. Then apply forward checking after placing the first queen in row 1 of column 1. [5 pts]
    Solution

    Variables Q₁…Q₄ (the row of the queen in each column), domains {1,2,3,4}, constraints for all i < j: Qᵢ ≠ Qⱼ and |Qᵢ − Qⱼ| ≠ j − i. After Q₁ = 1: Q₂ ∈ {3,4}, Q₃ ∈ {2,4}, Q₄ ∈ {2,3}.

  4. Why does MRV help even though it adds work at every step? [2 pts]
    Solution

    Choosing the most constrained variable makes failures surface at the top of the tree, where pruning a branch saves exponentially more work than the cheap bookkeeping costs.

Lecture 4 · Week 4 · Part I Classical AI

Logic and knowledge representation

How an agent can store what it knows as sentences and derive new facts mechanically, and why this precise approach struggles with the messy real world.

Learning objectives

  • Use the syntax and semantics of propositional logic; decide entailment with truth tables.
  • Convert sentences to conjunctive normal form and prove entailment by resolution.
  • Translate natural-language statements into first-order logic.
  • Compare symbolic knowledge representation with learned representations.

4.1 Knowledge-based agents

A knowledge-based agent keeps a knowledge base (KB) of sentences in a formal language. It tells the KB what it perceives and asks it what to do. The answers come from inference: deriving new sentences that follow from the KB. The appeal is that knowledge is explicit, inspectable and reusable for many questions.

4.2 Propositional logic

Sentences are built from proposition symbols (\(P\), \(Q\), \(Rain\)) with the connectives \(\neg\) (not), \(\wedge\) (and), \(\vee\) (or), \(\Rightarrow\) (implies) and \(\Leftrightarrow\) (if and only if). A model assigns true or false to every symbol.

Definition 4.1 (Entailment)

\(KB\) entails \(\alpha\), written \(KB \models \alpha\), if \(\alpha\) is true in every model in which \(KB\) is true. An inference procedure is sound if it derives only entailed sentences, and complete if it can derive every entailed sentence.

Model checking enumerates all \(2^n\) models of \(n\) symbols. That is sound and complete but exponential. Note that \(P \Rightarrow Q\) is false only when \(P\) is true and \(Q\) is false; in particular it is true whenever \(P\) is false.

Resolution

Every sentence can be rewritten in conjunctive normal form (CNF): a conjunction of clauses, each a disjunction of literals. The resolution rule combines two clauses that contain complementary literals:

\[ \frac{A \vee B \qquad \neg B \vee C}{A \vee C} \]

To prove \(KB \models \alpha\), add \(\neg\alpha\) to the KB and resolve until the empty clause appears (a contradiction). Resolution is sound and refutation-complete for propositional logic. For the restricted case of Horn clauses (at most one positive literal, i.e. rules like \(A \wedge B \Rightarrow C\)), forward and backward chaining decide entailment in linear time; this is the basis of Prolog and rule engines.

Example 4.1 (Proof by resolution)

KB: \(Rain \Rightarrow Wet\), \(Rain\). Query: \(Wet\). Clauses: \(\neg Rain \vee Wet\), \(Rain\), and the negated query \(\neg Wet\). Resolving the first two gives \(Wet\); resolving with \(\neg Wet\) gives the empty clause. So \(KB \models Wet\).

4.3 First-order logic

Propositional logic cannot say "all humans are mortal" without one symbol per human. First-order logic (FOL) adds objects, predicates (relations between objects), functions and the quantifiers \(\forall\) (for all) and \(\exists\) (there exists).

Example 4.2 (Translations)
  • All humans are mortal: \(\forall x\; Human(x) \Rightarrow Mortal(x)\)
  • Some student passed every exam: \(\exists s\; Student(s) \wedge \forall e\; (Exam(e) \Rightarrow Passed(s,e))\)
  • Every student has a supervisor: \(\forall s\; Student(s) \Rightarrow \exists p\; Supervises(p,s)\)

A common error: \(\forall\) goes with \(\Rightarrow\), \(\exists\) goes with \(\wedge\). The sentence \(\exists x\; Human(x) \Rightarrow Mortal(x)\) is true as soon as one non-human exists.

Inference in FOL uses unification (finding substitutions that make two expressions identical) together with resolution. FOL entailment is semi-decidable: if the KB entails a sentence, a proof will eventually be found; if not, the search may never stop.

4.4 Knowledge representation today

Large symbolic systems live on as ontologies and knowledge graphs: Wikidata, medical terminologies such as SNOMED CT, product catalogues. They store facts as subject–predicate–object triples and are queried precisely.

Their weakness is the one that sank expert systems. Real-world categories are fuzzy, exceptions are everywhere ("birds fly", except penguins, except in cages…), and every fact must be entered by someone. Neural networks learn sub-symbolic representations instead, vectors with no explicit meaning per dimension, which capture fuzzy similarity well but cannot be inspected or guaranteed correct. Much current research, neuro-symbolic AI, tries to combine the two. In practice, LLM agents often do exactly this: the model translates a question into a SQL or SPARQL query, and a database answers it precisely.

Summary

  • Logic gives knowledge a precise meaning: \(KB \models \alpha\) means \(\alpha\) holds in every model of the KB.
  • Resolution on CNF is a sound and complete proof method for propositional logic; FOL adds quantifiers and is only semi-decidable.
  • Symbolic knowledge is precise but brittle and expensive to build; learned representations are robust but opaque.

Lecture 5 · Week 5 · Part II Uncertainty

Probability and Bayesian reasoning

Logic says true or false. Real agents face sensors that lie, rules with exceptions and worlds they cannot fully see. Probability is the calculus for reasoning with degrees of belief.

Learning objectives

  • Compute with joint, marginal and conditional probabilities.
  • Apply Bayes' rule, including the base-rate effect.
  • Read independence assumptions from a Bayesian network and compute simple queries.
  • Estimate parameters by maximum likelihood and MAP.

5.1 The basics

A random variable takes values with probabilities that sum to 1. The joint distribution \(P(X,Y)\) gives the probability of every combination of values. From it we get the marginal \(P(X) = \sum_y P(X, y)\) and the conditional probability

\[ P(X \mid Y) = \frac{P(X, Y)}{P(Y)}. \]

The product rule \(P(X,Y) = P(X \mid Y)\,P(Y)\) and the chain rule \(P(X_1,\dots,X_n) = \prod_i P(X_i \mid X_1,\dots,X_{i-1})\) follow directly. Two variables are independent if \(P(X,Y) = P(X)P(Y)\), and conditionally independent given \(Z\) if \(P(X,Y \mid Z) = P(X \mid Z)P(Y \mid Z)\).

Why not just use the joint table? For \(n\) binary variables it has \(2^n - 1\) free entries. At 30 variables that is a billion numbers, none of which you could estimate reliably.

5.2 Bayes' rule

Theorem 5.1 (Bayes' rule) \[ P(H \mid E) = \frac{P(E \mid H)\,P(H)}{P(E)}, \qquad P(E) = \sum_h P(E \mid h)\,P(h). \]

\(P(H)\) is the prior, \(P(E \mid H)\) the likelihood, and \(P(H \mid E)\) the posterior.

Bayes' rule turns causal knowledge ("how likely is this symptom given the disease?"), which is stable and easy to obtain, into diagnostic knowledge ("how likely is the disease given the symptom?"), which is what the agent needs.

Example 5.1 (The base-rate effect)

A disease affects 1 in 1,000 people. A test detects it 99% of the time and has a 5% false-positive rate. You test positive. Then

\[ P(D \mid +) = \frac{0.99 \cdot 0.001}{0.99 \cdot 0.001 + 0.05 \cdot 0.999} \approx 0.019. \]

Less than 2%. Most positives come from the 999 healthy people, because the prior is so small. Doctors and juries routinely get this wrong.

Naive Bayes

If we assume that the observed features \(E_1,\dots,E_n\) are conditionally independent given the class \(C\), then

\[ P(C \mid E_1,\dots,E_n) \propto P(C)\prod_i P(E_i \mid C). \]

This naive Bayes classifier needs only one small table per feature. The assumption is almost always false (the words "free" and "money" are correlated in spam), yet the classifier often works well, because it only needs to rank classes correctly, not to get the probabilities right. It powered the first generation of spam filters.

5.3 Bayesian networks

Definition 5.1 (Bayesian network)

A Bayesian network is a directed acyclic graph whose nodes are random variables, together with a conditional probability table \(P(X_i \mid \mathrm{Parents}(X_i))\) for each node. It represents the joint distribution

\[ P(X_1,\dots,X_n) = \prod_i P(X_i \mid \mathrm{Parents}(X_i)). \]

Each node is conditionally independent of its non-descendants given its parents. When every node has at most \(k\) parents, the network needs \(O(n \cdot 2^k)\) numbers instead of \(2^n\).

Example 5.2 (Burglary alarm)

Burglary → Alarm ← Earthquake; Alarm → JohnCalls; Alarm → MaryCalls. Five binary variables need 1 + 1 + 4 + 2 + 2 = 10 numbers instead of 31. To compute \(P(Burglary \mid JohnCalls, MaryCalls)\), sum the factored joint over the hidden variables Alarm and Earthquake (inference by enumeration). With the textbook numbers the answer is about 0.28.

Exact inference is NP-hard in general. Variable elimination makes it efficient for networks with simple structure; otherwise we use approximate inference by sampling.

5.4 Learning probabilities from data

Where do the numbers come from? Given data \(D\), the maximum likelihood estimate picks the parameters \(\theta\) that make the data most probable:

\[ \hat\theta_{\text{MLE}} = \arg\max_\theta P(D \mid \theta). \]

For a coin with \(h\) heads in \(n\) tosses, \(\hat\theta = h/n\). After 3 tosses with 3 heads the MLE claims the coin always lands heads, which is clearly overconfident. The maximum a posteriori (MAP) estimate includes a prior: \(\arg\max_\theta P(D \mid \theta)P(\theta)\). With a uniform-ish prior this amounts to adding pseudo-counts, e.g. \((h+1)/(n+2)\) (Laplace smoothing). We will meet this idea again as regularisation in lecture 6, and the MLE idea as the cross-entropy loss used to train every LLM.

Summary

  • Probability quantifies uncertainty; Bayes' rule turns causal into diagnostic knowledge, but small priors dominate.
  • Conditional independence makes large models tractable: naive Bayes and Bayesian networks factor the joint.
  • MLE fits parameters to data; MAP adds prior knowledge and prevents overconfidence on small samples.

Lecture 6 · Week 6 · Part III Machine learning

Machine learning fundamentals

Instead of programming the answer, we program a way to learn it from examples. This lecture sets out the vocabulary and the central problem: generalising to data the model has never seen.

Learning objectives

  • Distinguish supervised, unsupervised, self-supervised and reinforcement learning.
  • State empirical risk minimisation and explain overfitting, underfitting and the bias–variance trade-off.
  • Set up a sound evaluation with training, validation and test sets and appropriate metrics.
  • Describe linear and logistic regression as the simplest learned models.

6.1 Kinds of learning

  • Supervised learning: learn a function \(f: x \mapsto y\) from labelled pairs \((x_i, y_i)\). If \(y\) is a category, it is classification; if it is a number, regression.
  • Unsupervised learning: find structure in unlabelled data, e.g. clusters (k-means), low-dimensional projections (PCA) or the data distribution itself.
  • Self-supervised learning: create labels from the data itself, e.g. predict the next word of a text or a masked patch of an image. This is how LLMs are pre-trained, and it is why they can use the whole internet as training data.
  • Reinforcement learning: learn from rewards for actions (lecture 12).

6.2 Learning as optimisation

We choose a model class, functions \(f_\theta\) with parameters \(\theta\), and a loss function \(L(\hat y, y)\) that measures how wrong a prediction is. Training means empirical risk minimisation:

\[ \hat\theta = \arg\min_\theta \frac{1}{N}\sum_{i=1}^{N} L\big(f_\theta(x_i),\, y_i\big). \]

Common losses are the squared error \((\hat y - y)^2\) for regression and the cross-entropy \(-\log \hat p(y)\) for classification, where \(\hat p(y)\) is the probability the model assigns to the correct class. Minimising cross-entropy is exactly maximum likelihood estimation (lecture 5).

Linear and logistic regression

Linear regression predicts \(\hat y = w^\top x + b\). With squared loss it has a closed-form solution. Logistic regression is the simplest classifier: it squashes a linear score through the sigmoid \(\sigma(z) = 1/(1+e^{-z})\) to get a probability, \(\hat p(y=1 \mid x) = \sigma(w^\top x + b)\). Its decision boundary is a hyperplane. For \(K\) classes, the softmax generalises the sigmoid:

\[ \mathrm{softmax}(z)_k = \frac{e^{z_k}}{\sum_j e^{z_j}}. \]

A neural network classifier is the same thing with a learned, non-linear feature extractor in front (lecture 8). Every LLM ends in a softmax over its vocabulary.

6.3 Generalisation

What matters is not the training loss but the error on new data from the same distribution, the generalisation error.

Definition 6.1 (Overfitting and underfitting)

A model overfits when it fits the training data, including its noise, much better than new data: low training error, high test error. It underfits when it is too simple to capture the pattern: both errors are high.

Classically, expected test error decomposes as

\[ \text{error} = \text{bias}^2 + \text{variance} + \text{noise}. \]

Bias is error from wrong assumptions (a straight line through a parabola). Variance is sensitivity to the particular training sample. More flexible models have less bias and more variance: this is the bias–variance trade-off.

Double descent. Very large neural networks break the classical U-curve: past the point where they fit the training data perfectly, test error falls again as models grow. Why over-parameterised networks generalise is still an active research question.

Regularisation fights overfitting by preferring simpler models: an L2 penalty \(\lambda\lVert\theta\rVert^2\) on the weights (weight decay; the MAP estimate with a Gaussian prior), L1 for sparsity, early stopping, dropout, data augmentation, and above all more data.

6.4 Evaluating models honestly

Split the data three ways: a training set to fit parameters, a validation set to choose hyperparameters (model size, learning rate, \(\lambda\)), and a test set used once, at the end, to report performance. Every decision made by looking at test results leaks information and makes the reported number optimistic. With little data, use k-fold cross-validation: train \(k\) times, each time holding out a different fold.

Choose the metric to fit the task:

  • Accuracy misleads on imbalanced data: a fraud detector that always says "no fraud" is 99.9% accurate.
  • Precision = TP / (TP + FP): of the items flagged, how many are right. Recall = TP / (TP + FN): of the real positives, how many were found. F1 is their harmonic mean.
  • For LLMs, held-out loss or perplexity during training, and task benchmarks afterwards (lecture 14).

Summary

  • Supervised learning minimises an average loss over training examples; cross-entropy is maximum likelihood.
  • The goal is generalisation. Too simple underfits, too flexible overfits; regularisation and more data help.
  • Keep the test set untouched until the end, and pick metrics that fit the cost of each kind of error.

Lecture 7 · Week 7 · Part III Machine learning

Optimisation and gradient descent

Almost every modern model, from logistic regression to a 400-billion-parameter LLM, is trained by the same simple idea: take small steps downhill on the loss.

Learning objectives

  • Compute gradients of simple losses and perform gradient descent steps by hand.
  • Explain the role of the learning rate, mini-batches and stochastic gradients.
  • Describe momentum and Adam and why they help.
  • Recognise common training failures from the loss curve.

7.1 Gradient descent

The gradient \(\nabla_\theta L\) is the vector of partial derivatives of the loss with respect to each parameter. It points in the direction of steepest ascent. Gradient descent repeatedly steps the other way:

\[ \theta_{t+1} = \theta_t - \eta\,\nabla_\theta L(\theta_t), \]

where the learning rate \(\eta\) sets the step size. Too small and training crawls; too large and the loss oscillates or diverges.

Example 7.1 (One step by hand)

Fit \(\hat y = wx\) to the single point \((x, y) = (2, 4)\) with squared loss \(L = (wx - y)^2\). Then \(\partial L / \partial w = 2(wx - y)x\). At \(w = 0\): gradient \(= 2 \cdot (0 - 4) \cdot 2 = -16\). With \(\eta = 0.05\): \(w \leftarrow 0 + 0.8 = 0.8\). Next step: gradient \(= 2(1.6 - 4)\cdot 2 = -9.6\), \(w = 1.28\). The weight approaches the optimum \(w = 2\). With \(\eta = 0.3\), the first step would jump to \(w = 4.8\) and overshoot ever further: divergence.

For convex losses (linear and logistic regression) gradient descent reaches the global minimum. Neural network losses are non-convex, with many local minima and saddle points, but in very high dimensions most local minima turn out to be about as good as each other, and plain gradient methods work remarkably well.

7.2 Stochastic and mini-batch gradient descent

Computing the exact gradient needs a pass over the whole training set, which is far too slow for millions of examples. Stochastic gradient descent (SGD) estimates it from a random mini-batch of, say, 32 to a few thousand examples. The estimate is noisy but unbiased, and much cheaper, so we can take many more steps. The noise even helps escape sharp minima, which tend to generalise poorly. One pass over the training data is an epoch.

Batch size and hardware. GPUs are efficient on large batches, so batch size is usually set by memory. When it grows, the learning rate is often scaled up with it.

7.3 Better optimisers

Momentum keeps a running average of past gradients and steps along that, like a ball rolling downhill. It speeds up progress along consistent directions and damps oscillation across narrow valleys:

\[ v_{t+1} = \beta v_t + \nabla L(\theta_t), \qquad \theta_{t+1} = \theta_t - \eta\, v_{t+1}. \]

Adam (adaptive moment estimation) additionally keeps a running average of squared gradients and divides each parameter's step by its square root. Parameters with large, noisy gradients get smaller steps, and rarely updated ones get larger steps. Adam with weight decay (AdamW) is the default for training Transformers.

Learning-rate schedules

Large models are trained with a short warm-up (the learning rate rises linearly from zero for the first few thousand steps, avoiding instability while Adam's statistics settle), followed by decay, typically cosine, towards a small final value.

7.4 Reading loss curves

  • Loss explodes or becomes NaN: learning rate too high, missing warm-up, or numerical overflow. Lower the rate; clip gradients to a maximum norm.
  • Loss flat from the start: learning rate too low, a bug in the data pipeline, or dead activations.
  • Training loss falls, validation loss rises: overfitting (lecture 6).
  • Can't overfit a single small batch: a bug. Checking that the model can memorise 10 examples is the first sanity test of any new training script.

Summary

  • Gradient descent steps against the gradient; the learning rate is the most important hyperparameter.
  • Mini-batch SGD trades exact gradients for many cheap, noisy steps.
  • Momentum and Adam, plus warm-up and decay, make training large networks stable and fast.

  1. For \(L(w) = (w-3)^2\), find the range of learning rates for which gradient descent converges. What happens at \(\eta = 0.5\)? [4 pts]
    Solution

    Update: w ← w − 2η(w − 3), so w − 3 ← (1 − 2η)(w − 3). It converges iff |1 − 2η| < 1, i.e. 0 < η < 1. At η = 0.5 the factor is 0: it jumps to the minimum in one step. For 0.5 < η < 1 it oscillates around the minimum but still converges.

    Solution

  3. A training set has 1.2 million examples and the batch size is 256. How many steps is one epoch? How many updates does full-batch gradient descent make per epoch? [2 pts]

Lecture 8 · Week 8 · Part III Machine learning

Neural networks and backpropagation

Stack simple units in layers, add non-linearities, and train every weight at once with the chain rule. This is the engine of all of deep learning.

Learning objectives

  • Compute the forward pass of a multilayer perceptron.
  • Derive and run backpropagation on a small network.
  • Explain the role of activation functions, initialisation, normalisation and dropout.

8.1 From neurons to networks

An artificial neuron computes a weighted sum of its inputs plus a bias and passes it through an activation function: \(a = g(w^\top x + b)\). Rosenblatt's perceptron (1958) used a step function and could learn any linearly separable classification. Minsky and Papert showed in 1969 that a single perceptron cannot learn XOR, which contributed to the first AI winter.

The fix is to stack layers. A multilayer perceptron (MLP) with layers \(\ell = 1, \dots, L\) computes

\[ h^{(0)} = x, \qquad h^{(\ell)} = g\big(W^{(\ell)} h^{(\ell-1)} + b^{(\ell)}\big), \qquad \hat y = W^{(L)} h^{(L-1)} + b^{(L)}. \]

Without the non-linearity \(g\), the layers would collapse into one linear map. With it, an MLP is a universal approximator: a single hidden layer with enough units can approximate any continuous function on a bounded domain. In practice deep networks need far fewer units than shallow ones for the same function, because they compose features hierarchically.

Activation functions

  • Sigmoid and tanh: smooth, but they saturate. For large inputs their derivative is near zero, which blocks learning in deep networks (vanishing gradients).
  • ReLU \(\max(0, z)\): cheap, does not saturate for positive inputs. The default since about 2011.
  • GELU and SwiGLU: smooth relatives of ReLU used in Transformers. Qwen, Llama and most current LLMs use SwiGLU in their feed-forward layers.

8.2 Backpropagation

To train with gradient descent we need \(\partial L / \partial W\) for every weight. Backpropagation computes all of them in one backward sweep through the network, applying the chain rule layer by layer and reusing intermediate results. Its cost is about twice that of the forward pass, independent of how many parameters there are.

Write \(z^{(\ell)} = W^{(\ell)} h^{(\ell-1)} + b^{(\ell)}\) and \(\delta^{(\ell)} = \partial L/\partial z^{(\ell)}\). Then

\[ \delta^{(\ell)} = \big(W^{(\ell+1)\top}\delta^{(\ell+1)}\big)\odot g'\big(z^{(\ell)}\big), \qquad \frac{\partial L}{\partial W^{(\ell)}} = \delta^{(\ell)}\, h^{(\ell-1)\top}, \qquad \frac{\partial L}{\partial b^{(\ell)}} = \delta^{(\ell)}. \]
Example 8.1 (Backprop on a tiny network)

One input \(x = 1\), one hidden ReLU unit with weight \(w_1 = 0.5\), output weight \(w_2 = 2\), target \(y = 3\), loss \(L = \tfrac12(\hat y - y)^2\).

Forward: \(z_1 = 0.5\), \(h = 0.5\), \(\hat y = 1\), \(L = 2\).
Backward: \(\partial L/\partial \hat y = \hat y - y = -2\). \(\partial L/\partial w_2 = -2 \cdot h = -1\). \(\partial L/\partial h = -2 \cdot w_2 = -4\); ReLU passes it through since \(z_1 > 0\), so \(\partial L/\partial w_1 = -4 \cdot x = -4\).
With \(\eta = 0.1\): \(w_1 = 0.9\), \(w_2 = 2.1\); the new output is \(1.89\), closer to 3.

Modern frameworks (PyTorch, JAX) do this automatically: automatic differentiation records the operations of the forward pass as a graph and runs the chain rule backwards through it.

History. The algorithm was found several times; Rumelhart, Hinton and Williams' 1986 paper made it widely known and ended the first neural-network winter.

8.3 Making deep networks trainable

  • Initialisation: weights drawn with variance about \(2/n_{\text{in}}\) (He initialisation for ReLU) keep activations and gradients at a stable scale from layer to layer. All-zero weights would make every unit in a layer identical forever.
  • Normalisation: batch normalisation (CNNs) or layer normalisation and RMSNorm (Transformers) rescale activations inside the network, allowing higher learning rates and deeper models.
  • Residual connections \(h + F(h)\) give the gradient a direct path through many layers (lecture 9).
  • Dropout: during training, randomly zero a fraction of activations. The network cannot rely on any single unit, which regularises it.

Summary

  • An MLP alternates linear maps and non-linearities; depth lets it build features hierarchically.
  • Backpropagation is the chain rule applied efficiently, at about twice the cost of a forward pass.
  • Careful initialisation, normalisation, residual connections and dropout make deep nets train well.

Deep architectures: CNNs, RNNs and embeddings

A plain MLP ignores the structure of its input. Convolutional networks exploit the grid structure of images, recurrent networks the order of sequences, and embeddings turn discrete symbols into vectors a network can work with.

9.1 Inductive bias

Every architecture builds in assumptions about its data, its inductive bias. A good bias lets the model learn from less data; a wrong one caps what it can learn. CNNs assume that nearby pixels belong together and that a cat is a cat wherever it is in the image. RNNs assume that order matters. Transformers (lecture 10) assume very little and compensate with enormous amounts of data.

9.2 Convolutional neural networks

A convolutional neural network slides small learned filters (kernels), typically \(3\times3\), over the image. Each filter computes a weighted sum of a local patch at every position, producing a feature map. Because the same filter is used everywhere (weight sharing), a conv layer has very few parameters and detects a pattern regardless of its position.

Definition 9.1 (Output size of a convolution)

For input width \(W\), kernel size \(K\), padding \(P\) and stride \(S\), the output width is \(\lfloor (W - K + 2P)/S \rfloor + 1\). A layer with \(C_{\text{in}}\) input and \(C_{\text{out}}\) output channels has \(C_{\text{out}}(K^2 C_{\text{in}} + 1)\) parameters.

Pooling (e.g. max over \(2\times2\) blocks) or strided convolutions shrink the feature maps, so deeper layers see larger regions of the image (a larger receptive field). Early layers learn edges and colours, middle layers textures and parts, late layers whole objects.

Residual networks

Simply stacking more layers made networks worse beyond about 20 layers, even on training data. ResNet (2015) added residual connections: each block computes \(h + F(h)\) instead of \(F(h)\). The block only has to learn a correction, and gradients flow straight through the identity path. Networks with over 100 layers became trainable. Every Transformer uses the same trick.

9.3 Recurrent networks

A recurrent neural network reads a sequence one element at a time and keeps a hidden state: \(h_t = \tanh(W_h h_{t-1} + W_x x_t + b)\). The same weights are used at every step. Training unrolls the loop and backpropagates through time.

Gradients through long sequences are products of many Jacobians and vanish or explode, so plain RNNs forget after a few dozen steps. The LSTM (1997) adds a cell state with gates that decide what to keep, forget and output, which carries information much further. LSTMs powered machine translation and speech recognition until about 2017.

Their remaining weakness is structural: step \(t\) cannot start until step \(t-1\) is done, so training cannot be parallelised along the sequence, and everything the model knows about the past must squeeze through one fixed-size vector. Attention removes both limits.

9.4 Embeddings

Networks need numbers, but words are discrete symbols. A one-hot vector (a 1 at the word's index) treats all words as equally different. An embedding maps each symbol to a dense vector of, say, 300 to 8,000 dimensions, learned so that symbols used in similar contexts get similar vectors.

Example 9.1 (Word2vec arithmetic)

Word2vec (2013) learned embeddings by predicting a word's neighbours. The result has geometric regularities: \(\vec{\text{king}} - \vec{\text{man}} + \vec{\text{woman}} \approx \vec{\text{queen}}\), and \(\vec{\text{Paris}} - \vec{\text{France}} + \vec{\text{Germany}} \approx \vec{\text{Berlin}}\). Similarity is measured by cosine similarity \(\cos(u,v) = u^\top v / (\lVert u\rVert \lVert v\rVert)\).

Embeddings are everywhere: the first layer of every LLM is an embedding table for its tokens, and sentence embedding models (such as the nomic-embed-text model in your LM Studio library) map whole passages to vectors for semantic search, the basis of retrieval-augmented generation in lecture 13.

Summary

  • Architectures encode inductive biases: locality and weight sharing for CNNs, order for RNNs.
  • Residual connections made very deep networks trainable.
  • RNNs and LSTMs process sequences step by step, which limits memory and parallelism.
  • Embeddings turn symbols into vectors whose geometry reflects meaning.

Attention and Transformers

One mechanism, attention, lets every position in a sequence look directly at every other. Built into the Transformer, it is the architecture behind every current language model.

Learning objectives

  • Explain multi-head attention, positional encodings and causal masking.

10.1 Attention

The idea: to compute a new representation for one position, take a weighted average of information from all positions, with weights that depend on how relevant each one is. Each position emits three vectors, computed by learned linear maps from its current representation: a query \(q\) (what am I looking for?), a key \(k\) (what do I contain?) and a value \(v\) (what do I pass on?).

Definition 10.1 (Scaled dot-product attention)

For query, key and value matrices \(Q, K, V\) with one row per position and key dimension \(d_k\),

\[ \mathrm{Attention}(Q,K,V) = \mathrm{softmax}\!\left(\frac{QK^\top}{\sqrt{d_k}}\right) V. \]

Row \(i\) of the softmax matrix holds the attention weights of position \(i\) over all positions; they are non-negative and sum to 1.

Dividing by \(\sqrt{d_k}\) keeps the dot products from growing with the dimension, which would push the softmax into saturation where gradients vanish. When \(Q\), \(K\) and \(V\) all come from the same sequence it is called self-attention.

Example 10.1 (Attention by hand)

Two positions with \(d_k = 2\). Query of position 1: \(q = (1, 0)\). Keys: \(k_1 = (1, 0)\), \(k_2 = (0, 1)\). Values: \(v_1 = (10, 0)\), \(v_2 = (0, 10)\).

Scores: \(q\cdot k_1/\sqrt2 = 0.707\), \(q \cdot k_2/\sqrt2 = 0\). Softmax: \(e^{0.707}/(e^{0.707}+1) = 0.67\) and \(0.33\). Output: \(0.67\,v_1 + 0.33\,v_2 = (6.7, 3.3)\). Position 1 attends mostly to itself because its query matches its own key.

Why "pronouns" is the classic example. In "The animal didn't cross the street because it was too tired", a trained model's attention from "it" puts high weight on "animal". Swap "tired" for "wide" and the weight moves to "street".

Multi-head attention

One attention pattern can only express one kind of relation at a time. Multi-head attention runs \(h\) attentions in parallel with separate, smaller projections (e.g. 32 heads of dimension 128 for a model width of 4,096), concatenates the results and projects them back. Different heads learn different relations: syntax, coreference, copying previous tokens. To save memory at inference time, many LLMs share keys and values between groups of query heads (grouped-query attention, GQA).

Position

Attention itself is order-blind: shuffle the input and the outputs shuffle the same way. Order must be injected via positional encoding. The original Transformer added sine and cosine waves of different frequencies to the embeddings. Current LLMs use rotary position embeddings (RoPE), which rotate queries and keys by an angle proportional to their position, so that the attention score depends on the relative distance. Extending RoPE (e.g. YaRN scaling) is how models reach context windows of hundreds of thousands of tokens.

10.2 The Transformer block

A Transformer is a stack of identical blocks. In the "pre-norm" form used by today's LLMs, each block is

\[ h' = h + \mathrm{MHA}(\mathrm{Norm}(h)), \qquad h'' = h' + \mathrm{FFN}(\mathrm{Norm}(h')). \]

The attention sublayer mixes information between positions; the feed-forward network (an MLP applied to each position separately, typically 3–4× wider than the model) processes information within each position, and holds most of the parameters. Residual connections and normalisation (lectures 8–9) keep a stack of 30 to 100+ blocks trainable.

The original 2017 Transformer had an encoder (reads the whole input, bidirectional attention) and a decoder (generates output). Today there are three families:

  • Encoder-only (BERT): understanding tasks, classification, embeddings.
  • Encoder–decoder (T5, the original): translation, summarisation.
  • Decoder-only (GPT, Llama, Qwen, Claude): text generation. Uses causal masking: position \(i\) may only attend to positions \(\le i\), implemented by setting the forbidden scores to \(-\infty\) before the softmax. This lets the model be trained on every next-token prediction in a sequence at once.

Mixture of experts

A mixture-of-experts (MoE) layer replaces the single FFN with many expert FFNs and a small router that sends each token to only a few of them. A model can then have a very large total parameter count but use only a fraction per token: qwen3-coder-30b-a3b has 30 billion parameters, of which about 3 billion are active per token. It needs the memory of a 30B model but runs at roughly the speed of a 3B one.

10.3 Cost

Self-attention compares every position with every other: for sequence length \(n\) and width \(d\) it costs \(O(n^2 d)\) time, and the score matrix needs \(O(n^2)\) memory per head. Doubling the context quadruples the attention cost. Efficient implementations (FlashAttention) avoid storing the full matrix, but the quadratic compute remains, which is why long prompts are slow to process on your own hardware. In return, all positions are processed in parallel during training, which is what made training on trillions of tokens feasible.

Summary

  • Attention is a softmax-weighted average of values, weighted by query–key similarity.
  • Multiple heads learn different relations; positional encodings (today RoPE) supply order.
  • A Transformer block is attention plus a per-position MLP, each wrapped in a residual connection with normalisation.
  • Decoder-only models with causal masking are the basis of LLMs; MoE makes them cheaper per token.
  • Attention costs grow quadratically with context length.

Large language models

From a Transformer that predicts the next token to an assistant that follows instructions, reasons and calls tools: how LLMs are built, aligned and served. Examples use the models running on your own BosGameM5.

11.1 Tokens

LLMs do not see characters or words but tokens, pieces from a fixed vocabulary of typically 32,000 to 256,000 entries. The vocabulary is built with byte-pair encoding (BPE): start from single bytes and repeatedly merge the most frequent adjacent pair into a new token. Common words become single tokens; rare words split into pieces ("Vorlesungsskript" might become "Vor", "les", "ungs", "skript"). In English, one token is about ¾ of a word; German and code need more tokens per word.

Why LLMs miscount letters. The model sees "strawberry" as two or three tokens, not ten letters, so counting the r's requires knowledge it was never directly shown.

11.2 Pre-training

A decoder-only Transformer is trained to predict the next token. For a text \(x_1, \dots, x_T\) the loss is the average cross-entropy

\[ L = -\frac{1}{T}\sum_{t=1}^{T} \log p_\theta(x_t \mid x_1,\dots,x_{t-1}). \]

Its exponential, \(e^{L}\), is the perplexity: the effective number of tokens the model is choosing between at each step. This is pre-training, self-supervised learning on trillions of tokens of web text, books and code. To predict the next token well across all of that, the model must pick up grammar, facts, reasoning patterns and programming. The result is a base model: a powerful text continuer, but not yet an assistant.

Scaling laws

Kaplan et al. (2020) found that loss falls as a smooth power law in model size \(N\), data \(D\) and compute \(C \approx 6ND\) FLOPs, over many orders of magnitude. These scaling laws let labs predict a large model's loss from small runs. The Chinchilla study (2022) showed that for a fixed compute budget, parameters and tokens should grow together, at about 20 tokens per parameter. Current models are trained well past that point (Llama 3 8B saw 15 trillion tokens, about 1,900 per parameter), because a smaller model trained longer is cheaper to serve.

Some abilities appear to emerge suddenly at scale; others improve smoothly but are measured with all-or-nothing metrics that make them look sudden.

11.3 From base model to assistant

  1. Supervised fine-tuning (SFT), also called instruction tuning: continue training on curated (instruction, good response) conversations in a chat format. The model learns the assistant role and the format.
  2. Preference optimisation: humans (or models) compare pairs of responses. RLHF trains a reward model on these preferences, then optimises the LLM against it with reinforcement learning (lecture 12). DPO skips the reward model and optimises directly on the preference pairs with a classification-style loss.
  3. Reinforcement learning on verifiable tasks: reward correct answers to maths problems and passing unit tests. This produced reasoning models that write a long chain of thought before answering. The "thinking" blocks you see from Qwen3 and gpt-oss in Claude Code are this.

Fine-tuning for your own purposes rarely updates all weights. LoRA freezes the model and trains small low-rank matrices added to some weight matrices, often less than 1% of the parameters. thinkingcap-qwen3.8-27b in your library is a community fine-tune of Qwen.

11.4 Generating text

Generation is a loop: run the model, get a probability distribution over the vocabulary for the next token, choose a token, append it, repeat. How to choose is the decoding strategy:

  • Greedy: always the most likely token. Deterministic but repetitive.
  • Temperature \(T\): divide the logits by \(T\) before the softmax. \(T < 1\) sharpens the distribution (more focused), \(T > 1\) flattens it (more varied), \(T \to 0\) is greedy.
  • Top-p (nucleus) sampling: sample only from the smallest set of tokens whose probabilities sum to \(p\), e.g. 0.95, cutting off the long tail of unlikely tokens. Top-k does the same with a fixed count.

Because every output is a sample, an LLM can state false things fluently, so-called hallucinations: the model is trained to produce plausible continuations, and plausible is not the same as true.

11.5 Serving LLMs on your own hardware

Generation has two phases. Prefill processes the whole prompt in parallel (compute-bound; the "47 tokens per second of prompt processing" you measured). Decode then generates one token at a time; each step must read all weights from memory, so its speed is bounded by memory bandwidth.

Example 11.1 (Decode speed on BosGameM5)

Strix Halo has about 256 GB/s memory bandwidth. Qwen3.8-27B at Q4_K_M is about 17.4 GB. Upper bound: \(256 / 17.4 \approx 15\) tokens/s. An MoE model with 3B active parameters reads only about 2 GB per token, so it can run five to ten times faster: this is why MoE models are popular for local inference.

The KV cache

During decode, the keys and values of all earlier tokens do not change, so they are stored rather than recomputed: the KV cache. Its size per token is

\[ 2 \times n_{\text{layers}} \times n_{\text{kv-heads}} \times d_{\text{head}} \times \text{bytes per value}. \]
Example 11.2 (Why context costs memory)

Take a typical configuration for a dense ~27B model: 64 layers, 8 KV heads (GQA) of dimension 128, 16-bit values. That is \(2 \cdot 64 \cdot 8 \cdot 128 \cdot 2 = 262{,}144\) bytes, i.e. 256 KiB per token. A full context window of 262,144 tokens then needs 64 GiB of KV cache, almost four times the weights. (Check the model's config.json for the real values; llama.cpp can also store the cache at 8 bits to halve it.) With 4 parallel slots, LM Studio splits that budget between them.

Quantisation

Quantisation stores weights with fewer bits. A 27B model needs 54 GB at 16 bits, about 29 GB at 8 bits, and about 16–17 GB at Q4_K_M, a llama.cpp scheme that averages roughly 4.8 bits per weight by storing small blocks of 4-bit integers with shared scale factors. Quality loss at 4–5 bits is small for large models; below 3 bits it grows quickly. The GGUF files in your LM Studio library are quantised this way.

Summary

  • LLMs are decoder-only Transformers pre-trained on next-token prediction over trillions of tokens; loss scales predictably with size, data and compute.
  • SFT, preference optimisation (RLHF, DPO) and RL on verifiable tasks turn a base model into an assistant and a reasoner.
  • Decoding samples from the predicted distribution; temperature and top-p control randomness.
  • Local inference is limited by memory bandwidth (decode speed) and memory size (weights plus KV cache); quantisation and MoE help with both.

12.1 Markov decision processes

Definition 12.1 (MDP)

A Markov decision process consists of states \(S\), actions \(A\), transition probabilities \(P(s' \mid s, a)\), a reward function \(R(s, a, s')\) and a discount factor \(\gamma \in [0, 1)\). The Markov property says that the next state depends only on the current state and action, not on the history.

A policy \(\pi(a \mid s)\) says what to do in each state. The goal is a policy that maximises the expected return, the discounted sum of rewards \(G_t = r_{t+1} + \gamma r_{t+2} + \gamma^2 r_{t+3} + \dots\). Discounting makes infinite sums finite and prefers rewards sooner rather than later.

The value function \(V^\pi(s)\) is the expected return from \(s\) when following \(\pi\); the action-value \(Q^\pi(s,a)\) is the expected return after taking \(a\) in \(s\) and then following \(\pi\).

12.2 Bellman equations and dynamic programming

The optimal values satisfy the Bellman optimality equation:

\[ V^*(s) = \max_a \sum_{s'} P(s' \mid s,a)\,\big[R(s,a,s') + \gamma V^*(s')\big]. \]

The value of a state is the best immediate reward plus the discounted value of where you end up. If the model \(P, R\) is known, value iteration turns this equation into an update and repeats it until the values converge; the optimal policy then picks the maximising action in each state.

Example 12.1 (A three-state corridor)

States A – B – C; C is terminal with reward +10 on entering it; each move costs −1; moves are deterministic; \(\gamma = 0.9\). Starting from \(V = 0\): after one iteration \(V(B) = 9\) (step to C: \(-1 + 10\)), \(V(A) = -1\). After two, \(V(A) = -1 + 0.9 \cdot 9 = 7.1\). The values then stay fixed: the policy "move right" is optimal.

12.3 Learning without a model: Q-learning

Usually the agent does not know \(P\) and \(R\); it must learn by acting. Q-learning keeps a table of estimates \(Q(s,a)\) and, after each transition \((s, a, r, s')\), moves the estimate towards the observed reward plus the best estimated future:

\[ Q(s,a) \leftarrow Q(s,a) + \alpha\big[r + \gamma \max_{a'} Q(s',a') - Q(s,a)\big]. \]

The bracket is the temporal-difference error. Q-learning converges to \(Q^*\) if every state–action pair is tried often enough, which raises the exploration–exploitation dilemma: exploit the best known action, or explore one that might be better? The simplest answer is \(\varepsilon\)-greedy: act randomly with probability \(\varepsilon\), otherwise greedily.

For large state spaces, a neural network replaces the table. DeepMind's DQN (2015) learned to play 49 Atari games from raw pixels this way.

12.4 Policy gradients and RL for LLMs

Instead of learning values, we can parameterise the policy \(\pi_\theta\) directly and follow the gradient of expected return. The policy gradient theorem gives

\[ \nabla_\theta J(\theta) = \mathbb{E}_{\pi_\theta}\big[\nabla_\theta \log \pi_\theta(a \mid s)\; \hat A(s,a)\big], \]

where the advantage \(\hat A\) measures how much better an action did than expected: make good actions more likely, bad ones less likely. PPO adds a limit on how far each update may move the policy.

An LLM is a policy: the state is the text so far, the action is the next token. In RLHF, a complete response gets a score from a reward model trained on human preferences, and PPO (or simplified variants like GRPO) adjusts the model to produce higher-scoring responses, with a penalty for drifting too far from the SFT model. With verifiable rewards (correct maths answer, tests pass) this is how reasoning models learned to think step by step.

Reward hacking. An agent optimises the reward you wrote, not the one you meant. A coding model rewarded for passing tests may learn to edit the tests. Lecture 14 returns to this.

Summary

  • MDPs formalise sequential decisions; policies are judged by expected discounted return.
  • The Bellman equation links a state's value to its successors'; value iteration solves known MDPs.
  • Q-learning learns from experience without a model, balancing exploration and exploitation.
  • Policy gradients optimise the policy directly; RLHF and RL with verifiable rewards apply them to LLMs.

Back to lecture 1's agents, built from LLMs: a model in a loop that reads observations, calls tools and acts. This is how Claude Code works, including when it runs on your own LiteLLM router.

Learning objectives

13.1 The agent loop

An LLM on its own only maps text to text. An LLM agent wraps it in a loop:

messages = [system_prompt, user_request]
while True:
    reply = llm(messages, tools=tool_schemas)
    if not reply.tool_calls:
        return reply.text                  # done: answer the user
    for call in reply.tool_calls:
        result = run_tool(call.name, call.arguments)
        messages.append(tool_result(call.id, result))

In terms of lecture 1: the LLM is the agent program, the message list is the internal state, tool calls are the actuators and tool results are the percepts. The ReAct pattern (2023) made this explicit: interleave a reasoning step, an action and an observation.

Function calling

With tool use (function calling), the application sends the model a list of tools, each with a name, a description and a JSON Schema for its arguments. The model, fine-tuned for this, answers with a structured call such as {"name": "Bash", "arguments": {"command": "git status"}} instead of text. The application runs it and sends back the result. The model never executes anything itself. When you looked at the LM Studio server log, the long list of JSON schemas (SendMessage, Skill, TaskStop) was exactly this: Claude Code's tool definitions sent with every request.

MCP. The Model Context Protocol standardises how tools are offered to agents, so a tool server (a database, a calendar, a browser) written once works with any MCP-capable agent.

13.2 Retrieval-augmented generation

A model's knowledge is frozen at training time and cannot include your private documents. Retrieval-augmented generation (RAG) retrieves relevant text at question time and puts it in the prompt:

  1. Index (once): split documents into chunks of a few hundred tokens with some overlap; compute an embedding for each chunk; store them in a vector database.
  2. Retrieve: embed the question; find the \(k\) chunks with the highest cosine similarity (approximate nearest-neighbour search makes this fast for millions of chunks). Combining it with keyword search (BM25), so-called hybrid search, catches exact names and numbers that embeddings miss.
  3. Generate: prompt the LLM with the question and the retrieved chunks, and ask it to answer from them and cite its sources.

RAG reduces hallucination and makes answers traceable, but its quality is capped by retrieval: if the right chunk is not retrieved, the model cannot use it. In agentic RAG, the model decides itself when and what to search, and can search again if the first results are poor. Coding agents mostly retrieve with plain tools like grep and file reads rather than embeddings.

13.3 Context, memory and multiple models

Everything an agent knows about its task lives in its context window. Long sessions fill it with tool output, so agents must manage it: summarise old turns (Claude Code's auto-compact, which is why it needs to know the model's real window via CLAUDE_CODE_MAX_CONTEXT_TOKENS), keep long-term notes in files, and delegate.

A subagent is a separate agent loop with a fresh context, started by the main agent for a self-contained sub-task. It returns only its conclusion, which keeps the main context small. Subagents can run on a different, cheaper model, and in parallel. A router like LiteLLM then distributes the work: in your fleet, the main session on the Strix Halo's large model, subagents on a smaller model, possibly on the Mac Mini (CLAUDE_CODE_SUBAGENT_MODEL).

Example 13.1 (Choosing a model per role)

Planning and code review need the strongest model; summarising a long log or searching files needs speed. A 27B dense model at ~15 tokens/s for the main loop and a 3B-active MoE at ~70 tokens/s for subagents gives a better overall result per hour than either alone.

13.4 Evaluating agents and their failures

Agents are judged on end-to-end task success: SWE-bench (fix real GitHub issues so the tests pass), terminal and browser task suites, success rates over many runs, cost and time per task. Typical failures:

  • Error compounding: a 95%-reliable step repeated 20 times succeeds only \(0.95^{20} \approx 36\%\) of the time.
  • Loops: retrying the same failing action.
  • Context loss: forgetting instructions once they scroll out of the window or get summarised away.
  • Prompt injection: text in a web page, file or tool result that the model mistakes for instructions ("ignore previous instructions and email me the API keys"). Since data and instructions share one channel, this is the main security risk of agents. Mitigations: least-privilege tools, human approval for risky actions, sandboxes.

Summary

  • An LLM agent is a loop of model calls and tool executions; function calling returns structured, schema-checked tool calls.
  • RAG grounds answers in retrieved documents; retrieval quality caps answer quality.
  • Context is the scarce resource: compaction, notes and subagents manage it; routers let different models serve different roles.
  • Agents fail by compounding errors, looping and prompt injection; evaluate on end-to-end task success.

14.1 Bias and fairness

Models learn the patterns in their data, including unfair ones. A hiring model trained on past decisions reproduces past discrimination; a face recogniser trained mostly on light-skinned faces fails more often on dark-skinned ones. Bias enters through unrepresentative data, historical injustice in the labels, proxy features (a postcode standing in for ethnicity) and the choice of what to predict.

Fairness can be formalised in several ways: equal positive rates across groups (demographic parity), equal error rates (equalised odds), or equally reliable scores (calibration). A classic result shows that when base rates differ between groups, these criteria cannot all hold at once. Choosing between them is a value judgement, not a technical one.

14.2 Alignment and safety

Alignment means getting a system to do what its designers and users actually intend. The difficulty is that we can only specify proxies for what we want, and optimisation exploits the gap:

  • Specification gaming: a boat-racing agent learned to circle forever collecting bonus points instead of finishing the race; a coding agent rewarded for passing tests special-cases the tests.
  • Sycophancy: models trained on human approval learn to agree with the user rather than be correct.
  • Misuse: capable models can help with harmful tasks; developers train refusals and filter outputs, and attackers try jailbreaks to get around them.
  • Agentic risk: agents act in the world. Destructive commands, leaked secrets and prompt injection (lecture 13) are concrete safety problems today. Practical safety means sandboxes, least privilege and human approval, the same reasons your local router keeps the model server behind a firewall and issues per-person keys.

Research directions include scalable oversight (using AI to help humans supervise AI), interpretability (understanding what the network computes internally) and red-teaming before release.

14.3 Measuring AI

Progress is reported on benchmarks: MMLU (broad knowledge, multiple choice), GSM8K and MATH (maths), HumanEval and SWE-bench (code), GPQA (graduate-level science), human preference rankings (Chatbot Arena). Read the numbers with care:

  • Contamination: benchmark questions leak into web-scale training data, so the model may have memorised answers.
  • Saturation: once top models score above 90%, the benchmark no longer separates them.
  • Goodhart's law: "When a measure becomes a target, it ceases to be a good measure."
  • Your use case is not the benchmark. For your own fleet, the best evaluation is a small set of tasks you actually do, run on each candidate model.

14.4 Regulation: the EU AI Act

The EU AI Act (in force since August 2024, with obligations phased in between 2025 and 2027) regulates AI by risk level:

  • Unacceptable risk, banned: social scoring by public authorities, manipulative techniques that exploit vulnerabilities, most real-time remote biometric identification in public spaces.
  • High risk: AI in hiring, education, credit scoring, critical infrastructure, law enforcement. Requires risk management, data governance, documentation, human oversight and conformity assessment.
  • Limited risk, transparency duties: people must know when they talk to a chatbot, and AI-generated content such as deepfakes must be labelled.
  • Minimal risk: spam filters, games; no new obligations.

Providers of general-purpose AI models have separate obligations: technical documentation, a copyright policy and a summary of training data, with additional evaluation and incident-reporting duties for the most capable models. Running open-weight models for personal use, as you do, is outside the scope of these provider duties.
