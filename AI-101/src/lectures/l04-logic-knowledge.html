Logic and knowledge representation

How an agent can store what it knows as sentences and derive new facts mechanically, and why this precise approach struggles with the messy real world.

Learning objectives

  • Use the syntax and semantics of propositional logic; decide entailment with truth tables.
  • Convert sentences to conjunctive normal form and prove entailment by resolution.
  • Translate natural-language statements into first-order logic.
  • Compare symbolic knowledge representation with learned representations.

4.1 Knowledge-based agents

A knowledge-based agent keeps a knowledge base (KB) of sentences in a formal language. It tells the KB what it perceives and asks it what to do. The answers come from inference: deriving new sentences that follow from the KB. The appeal is that knowledge is explicit, inspectable and reusable for many questions.

4.2 Propositional logic

Sentences are built from proposition symbols (\(P\), \(Q\), \(Rain\)) with the connectives \(\neg\) (not), \(\wedge\) (and), \(\vee\) (or), \(\Rightarrow\) (implies) and \(\Leftrightarrow\) (if and only if). A model assigns true or false to every symbol.

Definition 4.1 (Entailment)

\(KB\) entails \(\alpha\), written \(KB \models \alpha\), if \(\alpha\) is true in every model in which \(KB\) is true. An inference procedure is sound if it derives only entailed sentences, and complete if it can derive every entailed sentence.

Model checking enumerates all \(2^n\) models of \(n\) symbols. That is sound and complete but exponential. Note that \(P \Rightarrow Q\) is false only when \(P\) is true and \(Q\) is false; in particular it is true whenever \(P\) is false.

Resolution

Every sentence can be rewritten in conjunctive normal form (CNF): a conjunction of clauses, each a disjunction of literals. The resolution rule combines two clauses that contain complementary literals:

\[ \frac{A \vee B \qquad \neg B \vee C}{A \vee C} \]

To prove \(KB \models \alpha\), add \(\neg\alpha\) to the KB and resolve until the empty clause appears (a contradiction). Resolution is sound and refutation-complete for propositional logic. For the restricted case of Horn clauses (at most one positive literal, i.e. rules like \(A \wedge B \Rightarrow C\)), forward and backward chaining decide entailment in linear time; this is the basis of Prolog and rule engines.

Example 4.1 (Proof by resolution)

KB: \(Rain \Rightarrow Wet\), \(Rain\). Query: \(Wet\). Clauses: \(\neg Rain \vee Wet\), \(Rain\), and the negated query \(\neg Wet\). Resolving the first two gives \(Wet\); resolving with \(\neg Wet\) gives the empty clause. So \(KB \models Wet\).

4.3 First-order logic

Propositional logic cannot say "all humans are mortal" without one symbol per human. First-order logic (FOL) adds objects, predicates (relations between objects), functions and the quantifiers \(\forall\) (for all) and \(\exists\) (there exists).

Example 4.2 (Translations)
  • All humans are mortal: \(\forall x\; Human(x) \Rightarrow Mortal(x)\)
  • Some student passed every exam: \(\exists s\; Student(s) \wedge \forall e\; (Exam(e) \Rightarrow Passed(s,e))\)
  • Every student has a supervisor: \(\forall s\; Student(s) \Rightarrow \exists p\; Supervises(p,s)\)

A common error: \(\forall\) goes with \(\Rightarrow\), \(\exists\) goes with \(\wedge\). The sentence \(\exists x\; Human(x) \Rightarrow Mortal(x)\) is true as soon as one non-human exists.

Inference in FOL uses unification (finding substitutions that make two expressions identical) together with resolution. FOL entailment is semi-decidable: if the KB entails a sentence, a proof will eventually be found; if not, the search may never stop.

4.4 Knowledge representation today

Large symbolic systems live on as ontologies and knowledge graphs: Wikidata, medical terminologies such as SNOMED CT, product catalogues. They store facts as subject–predicate–object triples and are queried precisely.

Their weakness is the one that sank expert systems. Real-world categories are fuzzy, exceptions are everywhere ("birds fly", except penguins, except in cages…), and every fact must be entered by someone. Neural networks learn sub-symbolic representations instead, vectors with no explicit meaning per dimension, which capture fuzzy similarity well but cannot be inspected or guaranteed correct. Much current research, neuro-symbolic AI, tries to combine the two. In practice, LLM agents often do exactly this: the model translates a question into a SQL or SPARQL query, and a database answers it precisely.

Summary

  • Logic gives knowledge a precise meaning: \(KB \models \alpha\) means \(\alpha\) holds in every model of the KB.
  • Resolution on CNF is a sound and complete proof method for propositional logic; FOL adds quantifiers and is only semi-decidable.
  • Symbolic knowledge is precise but brittle and expensive to build; learned representations are robust but opaque.

Exercises (Sheet 4)

  1. Use a truth table to decide whether \((P \Rightarrow Q) \wedge (Q \Rightarrow R) \models (P \Rightarrow R)\). [4 pts]
    Solution

    Of the 8 models, the premise is true in (P,Q,R) = TTT, FTT, FFT, FFF. In all four, P ⇒ R is true. So the entailment holds (this is the transitivity of implication).

  2. Convert \(P \Leftrightarrow (Q \vee R)\) to CNF. [4 pts]
    Solution

    (P ⇒ Q ∨ R) ∧ (Q ∨ R ⇒ P) = (¬P ∨ Q ∨ R) ∧ (¬(Q ∨ R) ∨ P) = (¬P ∨ Q ∨ R) ∧ (¬Q ∨ P) ∧ (¬R ∨ P).

  3. Translate into FOL: (a) Every lecture has at least one student who attends it. (b) No student attends every lecture. [4 pts]
    Solution

    (a) ∀l Lecture(l) ⇒ ∃s Student(s) ∧ Attends(s,l). (b) ¬∃s Student(s) ∧ ∀l (Lecture(l) ⇒ Attends(s,l)), equivalently ∀s Student(s) ⇒ ∃l Lecture(l) ∧ ¬Attends(s,l).

  4. Give one task where a knowledge graph is clearly better than an LLM, and one where the reverse holds. [3 pts]
    Solution

    Knowledge graph: "List every drug that interacts with warfarin". It needs a complete, exact, auditable answer. LLM: "Summarise this patient's complaint from a free-text note". The input is fuzzy natural language with no fixed schema.
